## PDF page 2 — PROPYLENE GLYCOL (continued)

### How to avoid it

Read the complete ingredient list on the label of each product you use. Look for **propylene glycol** or one of its other names given above.

If no ingredient list is present, go to the store and inspect the original box or package. Some products’ ingredient lists are available on the internet at the manufacturer’s website, the store’s website, or at websites such as http://householdproducts.nlm.nih.gov, http://www.cosmeticsdatabase.com, or http://www.drugstore.com.

For possible workplace exposure, check the Material Safety Data Sheet (MSDS) and complete ingredient list of each product you encounter there. Use heavy duty gloves if you must contact propylene glycol.

Ask your doctor, nurse, attendant, hairdresser, manicurist, masseuse, aesthetician, and others to avoid using products containing propylene glycol in your care.

**NOTE:** You may also need to avoid the closely related chemicals **propylene glycol stearate** and **dipropylene glycol**. You will probably *not* cross-react to hexylene glycol or butylene glycol.

Glycerin may be a good substitute for some purposes.

### Foods that may contain propylene glycol

Some persons who are sensitive to propylene glycol notice a flare when they eat certain foods that contain the chemical. Your doctor may suggest you try a **one month** diet free of all such foods to see if you improve. You must **check all food ingredient labels** for propylene glycol.

## PDF page 3 — CHROMATE

Also includes chromium and its compounds, like **potassium dichromate**, ammonium dichromate, chromated zinc chloride, chromic acid, chromic nitrate, chromium dioxide, chromium hydroxide ultramarines, chromium phosphate, chromium oxide, chromium potassium sulfate, potassium bichromate, sodium dichromate, and zinc chromate.

### What is it?

Chromium is an element that is present in small amounts in many substances. Its many uses include for green color, to tan leather, to harden cement, and to make metal alloys.

### Where might it be found?

- Cosmetic pigments (mascara, eye shadow, eye liner, concealer, foundation, powder)
- Face scrub or mask
- Soap bars (e.g. Irish Spring)
- Shaving cream (prevents rust)

### Also in...

- Ceramic & artist pigments
- Tattoos (especially green)
- Textile dye fixing agent
- Roulette & pool table green felt
- Shoe polish
- Floor wax
- Chromic catgut and silk suture
- Orthopedic prostheses
- Acupuncture needles
- Orthodontic wires
- Dental fillings, implants
- Chrome-nickel dental crowns
- Detergents, esp. phosphate
- Photographic chemicals
- Photocopy paper, blueprints
- Engraving, lithography, offset
- Magnetic tapes
- Wet cement, mortar, bricksand
- Epoxy filler, spackle
- Drywall, primer, paint
- Pressure treated wood
- Fungicide in grout
- Veterinary fungicide
- Milk testing, lab test substance
- Gun & carburetor degreaser
- Leather, tanned (glove, golf glove, boots, shoes, garments, accessories, upholstery, briefcase, purse, wallet, belt, suede item, steering wheel)
- Metallurgy, electroplating, galvanizing, welding
- Antioxidant for antifreeze, radiators, pipelines
- Anticorrosive in dips, oils, fuels, drilling muds
- Explosives, fireworks, safety match heads
- Animal hide glues
- Liner in high temperature industrial furnaces

### How to avoid it

Avoid using any product that contains chromates. It is important to check the complete ingredient list of each product you use for a name listed above. Some products’ ingredient lists are available on the internet at the manufacturer’s website, the store’s website, or websites such as http://householdproducts.nlm.nih.gov, http://www.cosmeticsdatabase.com, or http://www.drugstore.com.

For possible workplace exposure, check the Material Safety Data Sheet (MSDS) and the ingredient labels of all solutions and products encountered at work.

Replace leather with vinyl, polyvinylchloride (e.g. Tingley rubber work boot), canvas (e.g. Ked), or synthetics. Or use aldehyde- or vegetable-tanned leather (e.g. www.mooshoes.com or vegetarian choices at www.zappos.com). Some Hush Puppies (800-545-2425), Birkenstock, and Ecco are chromate-free. Instead of leather gloves, wear heavy duty fabric, canvas, rubber, or vinyl. For dental crowns, use porcelain. For orthopedic prostheses, consider titanium.

### Workplace exposure note

**NOTE:** Problems at work can come from contact with the chromium fumes in processes like welding and electroplating. Seek protection by using an exhaust hood, respirator mask, or other means of proper ventilation or shielding.

## PDF page 5 — NICKEL

### What is it?

Nickel is a durable inexpensive metal that is a very common cause of allergic contact dermatitis. It is widely present in shiny silvery objects, and sometimes in water or products stored or cooked in metal. Allergy to nickel may develop in childhood and can persist for life.

### Where might it be found?

- jewelry, rings, necklace clasp
- buttons, snaps, zipper, hooks
- rivets, studs, suspender clips
- belt buckle, watch band buckle
- purse clasp, strap buckle
- hairpins, clips, barrettes
- eyelash curler, tweezers
- nail clippers, nail file
- razor blades, shaver screen
- lipstick holder, compact
- bra underwire, bra hook, clasp
- eyeglass frames
- dental implants, fillings, braces
- cigarette lighter
- keys, key-chain, key ring
- coins, Euro, money clip
- pocket knife
- cell phone, i-Pod
- badge chain or clip
- scissors, letter opener
- paper clips, staples
- metal pens, metal pencils
- stethoscope neckpiece
- acupuncture needles
- knitting needles
- sewing needle, pins, thimble
- kitchen utensils, cutlery
- tea ball, egg beater
- vacuum cleaner, toaster
- metal instruments, parts, file
- work tools, hobby tools
- chain saw or lathe fragments
- electrical wiring, water pipes
- plastics reagents
- enamel dyes
- duplicating fluids
- musical instruments, surgical instruments
- orthopedic metal implants, esp. static and large
- brushed chrome fixtures, cabinet handles, knobs
- pigments in fabric, ceramics, wallpaper, paint
- alkaline batteries, magnet cores, electroplate
- electronic chips
- cutting fluids, coolants, fuel additives
- smoke from cigarettes, foundries, incinerators
- insecticides

### How to avoid it

Many plated metals and alloys release nickel when in contact with sweat on the skin. Objects can be checked for nickel by swabbing with dimethylglyoxime and ammonium hydroxide. A bright pink shows nickel is present. You may buy a test kit (order from Allerderm, 800-365-6868, www.allerderm.com or Delasco, www.delasco.com).

*Transcription note: A handwritten star appears beside the preceding nickel-testing paragraph.*

Avoid nickel by carrying coins in plastic pouches. Cover keys with a plastic key guard or use copper keys. Use an emery board rather than nail file. Use silicone cases for cell phones and i-Pods. Cover nickel items with several coats of Nickel Guard (www.nickelsolution.com), clear nail polish, or acrylic paint, and reapply when it chips. A heavy cloth backing or iron-on patch on jeans behind a rivet or snap may help protect.

Select scissors and tools with plastic, rubber, or wood handles. Use vinyl gloves to handle metal objects or industrial solutions containing nickel. Reduce friction, pressure, and perspiration if exposure is required. Adults, but not children, may try protective creams (like Tetrix or 3% clioquinol, www.delrayderm.com). For prostheses and implants, consider materials like porcelain, titanium, or zirconium.

## PDF page 7 — LANOLIN

Also called: **wool alcohol, lanolin alcohol, wool wax, wool grease, wool fat,** or ***Adeps lanae anhydrous.***

### What is it?

Lanolin is a fat-like substance secreted by sheep oil glands to repel water from wool. Its content varies, but may include lanolin sterols, fatty alcohols, and fatty acids. It is often processed by acetylation, dewaxing, and hydrogenation, so that lanolin becomes a complex mixture. Thus, a person may *tolerate* one type of lanolin but react to another.

### Where might it be found?

- Creams, lotions, moisturizers
- Lipstick, lip balm
- Makeup, blush, concealer
- Eye liner, eye shadow
- Mascara, eyebrow pencil
- Makeup remover
- Cuticle cream
- Shaving cream
- Hair removal cream
- Hair conditioner, hairspray
- Soap, body wash, cleanser
- Sunscreen
- Sunless tanning preparation
- Nipple cream, Bag Balm
- Massage cream, liniment
- Diaper rash ointment
- Hemorrhoid preparation
- Wound care product or plaster
- Healing ointment
- Anti-itch or Cortisone cream

### Some other uses of lanolin

- Pet shampoos, grooming aids
- Furniture polish, wax
- Shoe polish
- Ski wax, boat wax
- Fur and leather waterproofing
- Impregnation agent for textiles
- Printing ink
- Wax and polish
- Insulation for wiring
- Cooling fluids for metalworking
- Metal lubricant, sealant
- Rust or corrosion inhibitor

### How to avoid it

Avoid using any product that contains lanolin. Inspect the label of each product you use to find the complete ingredient list. If lanolin is present, one of its names listed above should appear in the ingredients. If no ingredient list is present, go to the store and inspect the original box or package.

Some products’ ingredient lists are available on the internet at the manufacturer’s website, the store’s website, or at websites such as http://householdproducts.nlm.nih.gov, http://www.cosmeticsdatabase.com, or http://www.drugstore.com.

For possible workplace exposure, check the complete ingredient list of each product you encounter.

**Ask your doctor** to check the ingredients of the medications prescribed for use on your skin to be sure there is no lanolin present. Ask your hairdresser, barber, masseuse, and nurse to avoid using lanolin-containing products in your care. If you must come in contact with lanolin as you care for children, elders, or pets, wear protective gloves made of rubber, nitrile, or vinyl.

Rarely, lanolin sensitive persons will show cross-sensitivity to **cetyl alcohol** or **stearyl alcohol** also.

By reading ingredient labels, you should be able to avoid this allergen and find safe alternatives that are free of it. If you are uncertain about a particular skin product, ask your doctor about doing a Repeat Open Application Test with it.

Amerchol is an emollient and an emulsifier used in medicated ointments, furniture polish, waxes, textiles and inks. ## PDF page 9 — NEOMYCIN

### What is it?

Neomycin is an antibiotic used in many creams, ointments, powders, eye and ear medicines, bandages, and veterinary products.

### Where might it be found?

- Antibiotic cream
- Antibiotic ointment
- Triple antibiotic salve
- Bandage or gauze
- Pain relief preparation
- Wound care ointment
- Scar treatment
- Ulcer preparation
- Ear drops
- Swimmer’s ear drops
- Eye drops
- Eye ointment
- Nose drops
- Medicated powder
- Dentist root canal filling
- Urinary irrigant
- Pet food
- Veterinary animal feed

*(Some brand names are Neosporin, Cortisporin, Maxitrol, Neocidin, and Otocort.)*

### Notes

**Note:** Neomycin may remain in clothing even after laundering. Consider discarding items like knit socks, gloves, bras, two-ply garments, or shoes that have been against treated areas. They may harbor the drug and produce continued exposure.

Some people develop sensitivity to *bacitracin* along with their allergy to neomycin, although it is not chemically related. Ask your doctor if you should avoid bacitracin too.

### How to avoid it

Avoid any product that lists **neomycin** as an ingredient. Inspect the label, box, or package insert to find the complete list of ingredients for everything you use on your skin, eyes, or ears. Neomycin is present in many over-the-counter preparations at your drug store and grocery store. It may also be found in prescription products. **You must tell your doctors, dentist, nurse, and any caretakers about this allergy.** Show them the list of **safe choices below.**

**Your allergy to Neomycin means you could react to certain closely related antibiotics. You could develop a rash all over your body if you are given one of these by mouth or injection. DO NOT USE:**

- Tobramycin
- Gentamicin
- Butirosin
- Paromomycin
- Framycetin
- Kanamycin
- Amikacin
- Spectinomycin
- Streptomycin
- Dihydrostreptomycin
- Netilmicin
- Sisomicin
- Ribostamycin

### What can you use?

Your doctor can prescribe a different treatment that is safe for you to use for your infection. Some safe choices, depending on your condition, may include mupirocin (Bactroban), erythromycin (Ilotycin), retapamulin (Altabax), and silver sulfadiazine (Silvadene). Topical sulfonamides, fluoroquinolones, chloramphenicol, and tetracyclines may also be considered. For some problems, your doctor may recommend simply white petrolatum. You may be instructed how to use povidone-iodine, chlorhexidine, hydrogen peroxide, or compresses with aluminum acetate.

## PDF page 10 — BACITRACIN

### What is it?

Bacitracin is an antibiotic found in some products used on the skin, eyes, and ears.

### Where might it be found?

- Antibiotic creams
- Antibiotic ointments
- Bandages
- Ear drops
- Eye drops
- Eye ointments
- Gauze
- Pain relief preparations
- Powders
- Scar treatments
- Triple antibiotic salve
- Ulcer preparations
- Wound treatments

**You must tell your doctor, dentist, nurse, and any caretakers about this allergy. Show them the list of safe choices in the next column.**

### Warning printed in the handout

Bacitracin can produce **hives** in some people. This type of reaction can be dangerous. If you get hive-like welts, lip or face swelling, sweatiness, or shortness of breath from contact with bacitracin, seek immediate medical attention. If this happens, *absolutely* avoid any future exposure to bacitracin.

### How to avoid it

To avoid bacitracin, read the complete ingredient list on the label of every product you buy for your skin, eyes, or ears. This antibiotic is present in many over-the-counter preparations at your drug store and grocery store. It may also be in some prescription products.

Bacitracin may remain in clothing even after it is laundered. Consider discarding items you have worn against treated sites, like knit socks, gloves, bras, two-ply garments, or shoes, since such garments may keep exposing your skin to this allergen.

### What can you use?

Your doctor can prescribe a different treatment that is safe for you to use for infection.

Some safe choices, depending on your condition, may include mupirocin (Bactroban), erythromycin (Ilotycin), retapamulin (Altabax), and silver sulfadiazine (Silvadene). Topical sulfonamides, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, and chloramphenicol may also be considered.

Gentamicin cream or ointment is an acceptable alternative **if you are *not* also allergic to neomycin.**

For some problems, your doctor may recommend simply white petrolatum, or you may be told how to use povidone-iodine, chlorhexidine, hydrogen peroxide, or compresses with aluminum acetate.

## PDF page 11 — GOLD

Also called: **gold sodium thiosulfate, sodium aurothiosulfate, thiochrysine,** or **gold.**

### What is it?

Gold is a metallic element used in metal objects or dissolved in solutions. Its commonest use is in jewelry, but many other potential sources of exposure are listed below.

### Where might it be found?

- jewelry:
  - watches
  - rings
  - necklaces
  - bracelets
  - earrings
  - piercing studs
  - pin or brooch
- glasses frames
- buckle or clasp
- cosmetic case
- key chain or fob
- mirror and picture frame
- pill case
- gold coins
- gold medal, statue, crown
- bookbinding gold leaf
- thread in embroidery
- gold leaf or foil on chocolates
- gold leaf on baked goods
- dental appliances, bridge
- dental crown, prosthesis
- ceramic paint and enamel
- cranberry glass
- reflective layer in CDs
- electronic circuits
- gold solder
- electrical wiring, contacts
- protective coating in aircraft
- coating in electron microscopy
- toners for photography
- biological research
- materials science research
- heart stents (in the past)
- therapy for certain diseases

**Note:** Sometimes the source of contact with gold is found on the patient’s partner.

### How to avoid it

Often gold allergy shows up as a rash on the eyelids, face, or bridge of the nose. These sites may not be in direct contact with the metal. It is likely that harder substances like titanium dioxide and zinc oxide in products like sunscreens, make-up, and eye shadow scrape off small particles of gold that get transferred to the site of the rash. Always wash your hands well to avoid such transfer.

Gold may also produce hand rash. Split fingertips along with a red face may be a reaction to gold dental work. Gold dental work may also produce mouth sores alongside the metal.

For possible workplace exposure, check the complete ingredient list of each item encountered to look for gold or one of its other names listed above. Wear clean protective gloves if it is necessary to handle gold at work.

To avoid gold, select jewelry and glasses frames made of other metals, such as sterling silver or platinum. Solid copper, stainless steel, pewter, bronze, titanium, and brass are other alternatives. If a certain piece of gold jewelry must be worn, consult your jeweler about plating the item with rhodium or platinum. Ask the expected cost and required frequency of re-plating.

If you are allergic to gold, you should probably avoid gold dental work and surgical implants containing gold.

### What is Shellac and where is it found?

Shellac is a resin secreted by the female lac bug. It is processed for both industrial and food uses in four grades; orange, dewaxed orange, regular bleached and refined wax-free bleached.
